    <h2 id="how-we-ranked">How we ranked these firms</h2>
    <p>Personal injury is the most heavily advertised area of law in New York. Subway cars, taxi tops and daytime television are full of firms promising maximum compensation. That noise makes it hard to tell a genuine trial firm from a high-volume settlement mill. So we ignored advertising entirely and scored each of the 84 firms we reviewed on four weighted factors.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>Trial record (35%).</b> Jury verdicts in New York state and federal courts over the past five years, confirmed against court records — not self-reported "recovered" totals.</li>
      <li><b>Verified results (25%).</b> Settlements and verdicts we could substantiate through public filings, published decisions or documentation provided under our verification protocol.</li>
      <li><b>Client experience (25%).</b> Independent reviews weighted for recency, specificity and verified engagement, plus our own mystery-shopper intake calls.</li>
      <li><b>Peer recognition (15%).</b> Confidential evaluations from New York plaintiff and defense attorneys, and appellate work that shaped New York injury law.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>Every firm below has active licensure for all named attorneys and no public discipline in the past decade. Read the <a href="#">full methodology</a>.</p>

    <h2 id="what-to-look-for">What to look for in a New York personal injury firm</h2>
    <p>Rankings are a starting point. The right firm for a trucking case on the Cross Bronx Expressway is not the right firm for a misdiagnosis at a Manhattan hospital. As you narrow the list, weigh five things.</p>
    <h3>A trial record, not just a settlement history</h3>
    <p>Insurance carriers keep lists of which firms actually pick juries. They pay more, sooner, to the ones that do. Ask any firm for its verdicts in the past three years — in your county.</p>
    <h3>Independent recognition</h3>
    <p>Peer-reviewed recognition from other attorneys is far harder to buy than a billboard. Certification here requires it, but also ask about appellate decisions the firm has won; they shape the law for every case that follows.</p>
    <h3>Direct access to a senior attorney</h3>
    <p>In high-volume firms, your "lawyer" may be a case manager you never meet. Ask, before signing, who will handle your file, who will return your calls and how quickly.</p>
    <h3>A fee agreement you understand</h3>
    <p>New York caps most PI fees at one-third, and medical malpractice fees follow a statutory sliding scale. The variable is expenses: whether experts, depositions and filing fees are deducted before or after the fee is calculated can change your net recovery by tens of thousands of dollars.</p>
    <h3>Focus that matches your injury</h3>
    <p>A birth-injury case needs obstetric experts and a seven-figure case budget. A rideshare collision needs someone fluent in New York's no-fault and Serious Injury Threshold rules. Match the firm's verified focus to your facts.</p>

    <blockquote>"The best firms turn down more cases than they take. If a firm says yes before asking about your medical records, be cautious."<br><small class="muted" style="font-weight:400;font-size:.9rem">— A New York plaintiff's attorney interviewed for this ranking</small></blockquote>

    <h2 id="ny-law">New York personal injury law: the basics</h2>
    <p><b>Statute of limitations.</b> Three years from the date of injury for most negligence claims (CPLR §214), two years and six months for medical malpractice (CPLR §214-a), and two years for wrongful death, measured from the date of death.</p>
    <p><b>Claims against the government.</b> Injured by a city bus, in a NYCHA building or on a city sidewalk? You must serve a Notice of Claim within <b>90 days</b> and file suit within one year and 90 days. This is the single most common reason valid New York cases are lost.</p>
    <p><b>Comparative negligence.</b> New York follows pure comparative negligence: you can recover even if you were mostly at fault, with your award reduced by your percentage of responsibility.</p>
    <p><b>No-fault and the Serious Injury Threshold.</b> Motor vehicle injuries are first paid through no-fault insurance (up to $50,000 in basic economic loss). To sue for pain and suffering, you must show a "serious injury" as defined by Insurance Law §5102(d) — one reason experienced counsel matters in car cases.</p>
    <p><b>The Scaffold Law.</b> Labor Law §240(1) imposes absolute liability on owners and contractors for gravity-related construction injuries — a uniquely powerful tool for New York construction workers.</p>

    <h2 id="faq">Frequently asked questions</h2>
    <div class="faq">
      <details open><summary>How much does a personal injury lawyer cost in New York?</summary><div>Almost all New York PI firms work on contingency, typically 33⅓% of the recovery. Medical malpractice fees follow a statutory sliding scale (30% of the first $250,000 down to 10% above $1.25 million). You pay nothing up front and nothing if you don't recover.</div></details>
      <details><summary>How long do I have to file a personal injury lawsuit in New York?</summary><div>Generally three years from the injury, but only 2½ years for medical malpractice and just 90 days to serve a Notice of Claim against New York City, the MTA or NYCHA. Contact a firm as early as possible.</div></details>
      <details><summary>What is the average personal injury settlement in NYC?</summary><div>There is no meaningful "average" — outcomes range from a few thousand dollars for soft-tissue car injuries to eight-figure verdicts for catastrophic cases. Every firm on this list offers a free evaluation that will give you a realistic range for your facts.</div></details>
      <details><summary>Can I switch personal injury lawyers mid-case?</summary><div>Yes. In New York, your new firm and old firm split the single contingency fee based on work performed; it costs you nothing extra. Several firms on this list regularly take over cases from other attorneys.</div></details>
      <details><summary>Do I need a lawyer for a minor car accident?</summary><div>If your injuries resolved within a few weeks and no-fault covered your bills, possibly not. If you have ongoing treatment, missed work or a possible "serious injury," a free consultation is worth 30 minutes of your time.</div></details>
